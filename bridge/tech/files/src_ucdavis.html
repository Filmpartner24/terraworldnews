Making Caviar From Food Waste

3 ways public research strengthens the sustainable food industry

  • by Tatiana Olivera Muniz
  • November 17, 2026

By the time you reach for a handful of almonds, their hulls are long gone. For Cody Yothers and Josh Ansel, that’s where the story starts. Their startup, Optimized Foods, uses nutrients from almond hulls to grow tiny pearls of vegan caviar.

Yothers credits years of federally funded research at UC Davis with giving his team a place to start. Now their company is showing how food makers can get more from the crops farmers already grow.

Do small businesses need public research?

Yothers and Ansel have a clear answer: Yes, federally funded research is what made their food startup work. 

Located in Davis, California, the Optimized Foods team uses nutrients from agricultural byproducts (for example, almond hulls) to grow a type of mycelium called koji.

Mycelium is the root-like network that makes up fungus. Koji, the same fungi that makes sake and soy sauce, is safe to eat. Optimized Foods turns koji mycelium into edible food like tiny pearls of vegan caviar.

The founders chose to make caviar out of mycelium because its taste and texture would be hard to copy. They wanted to show what their approach could do.

But their starting point came from years of work at UC Davis.

“To have a solution that really works for our customers, we rely on innovation that comes out of decades of research initially funded by federal research dollars,” Yothers said.

Federal research grants are investments in new solutions that serve everyone.

Optimized Foods shows what federal investment can help make possible. Continued support gives the next useful idea a chance to help even more industries.

How can our research improve your life?

Visit From Labs to Lives to hear from people whose lives have changed from our discoveries and learn how you can support public research.

Here are three ways public research strengthens the sustainable food industry and supports small businesses like Optimized Foods.

1. Public research gives startups the knowledge needed to start

Before someone can build a business around a new food, they need to know how to make it.

UC Davis professor Ruihong Zhang and alum researcher Minami Ogawa know how to make mycelium.

Together, their research helped create key parts of the food technology that Optimized Foods and other food startups use today.

“What we’re doing is really innovative,” Yothers said. “And that innovation came from research at UC Davis.”

What makes this research so innovative?

Yothers says food sustainability is a top goal for Optimized Foods.

Food sustainability means growing, producing and eating food in a way that protects the environment, treats people and animals fairly, and ensures there is enough food for everyone in the future.

Food companies rely on public research to find new solutions to promote food sustainability.

Mycelium is considered a sustainable food in two ways:

  1. It can serve as an alternative food.
  2. It can upcycle food byproducts that would normally go to waste.

Alternative foods resemble familiar foods or ingredients, such as meat or dairy, while delivering a similar nutritional profile. Some are designed to provide more nutrients, cut calories or fat, use fewer natural resources, or cost less for producers to make and consumers to buy.

The USDA also supported Zhang’s 2025 fungi cultivation study to see how much larger batches of mycelium might cost. For the alternative food industry, costs are the biggest hurdle, so Zhang’s research findings matter. For example, the results can help founders decide which ideas to pursue before spending money on expensive equipment or a larger plant.

Also, public funding for work like Zhang’s helps create solutions that can serve a wide variety of alternative food producers, including alternative proteins, or meat substitutes.

2. Public research supports food sustainability by upcycling food byproducts

The sustainability goal in food production starts with a simple question: What else could we make from what we already grow?

That question is answered through research funded by federal grants. For example, a U.S. Department of Agriculture grant helped Zhang’s team test how to grow edible fungi from almond hulls.

Food byproducts are materials left over when food is made or processed.

Almond hulls are an example of an agricultural or food byproduct. They’re the outer coverings removed during almond processing. Optimized Foods uses nutrients from those hulls to grow edible fungi for its vegan caviar.

Think of all the resources that go into growing a crop, like land, water and labor. Finding more uses for a harvest could help us get more value from those resources. It could also give growers and processors another buyer for part of their crop. For food makers, it could provide a new source of ingredients.

Almond hulls already have sustainable uses, such as feeding livestock. But Optimized Foods is exploring another solution: helping grow food for people.

But getting there takes research, and research takes research funding. Zhang’s team is testing ways to make better use of almond hulls, with Optimized Foods as a partner.

Without federal funding, Zhang’s team will struggle to do the important work that helps innovative businesses like Yothers’.

Richer cocoa through fat in fungi

Maybe you don’t like caviar, but the work behind it could help improve more familiar foods like cocoa.

Optimized Foods is developing Cocoa Koji, an ingredient meant to give cocoa a richer taste and smoother feel. Another Optimized Foods ingredient, HYphat, is a solid fat ingredient containing a high composition of healthy fats. The company aims to offer new choices in place of animal, palm or coconut fats.

And while sustainability is a valuable goal, it isn’t the only one for Optimized Foods. Yothers says each ingredient still must meet the needs of food makers and the people eating their products.

Taste matters. So does cost. A new ingredient needs to work in a recipe and be something people want to buy again. But the only way to make sure that happens is to do research and development.

Public research helps businesses reach their goals. It gives them knowledge they can use to develop products, test ideas and decide what to improve next.

3. Public research builds career-ready graduates for the food tech industry

Public universities like UC Davis provide businesses with key knowledge that helps them invent and improve their products. They also equip businesses with career-ready graduates who are already experts in their field and require little training.

Our student researchers in food science are given hands-on opportunities to work alongside our expert researchers and business collaborators, getting them ready to enter the cutting-edge food technology industry.

In a 2025 future food challenge, students from UC Davis and Sacramento City College tested ways to make food from fungi. Zhang helped form the team, while Optimized Foods served as an industry advisor and provided hands on experience on their state-of-the-art fermenters.

For UC Davis student Matthew Tsang, the project deepened his interest in applying for graduate school. The students also visited companies to see how their work could lead to viable careers in the food tech industry.

The food challenge was run by BioMADE, an institute funded by the U.S. Department of Defense.

This is yet another way public research funding reaches beyond campus. Students gain skills they can take into future jobs, businesses gain a chance to work with well-trained new hires, and industries prosper.

Why federal support matters for food and agriculture

Federal support helps pay the direct and indirect costs for the people, tools and lab space behind important research. Cutting that support can mean fewer chances to test an innovative idea or train a student.

The 2027 USDA budget proposal would cut the annual funds by about 38%.

This is the fiscal year 2027 budget proposal Congress sets for the National Institute of Food and Agriculture. As of Oct. 23, 2026, the proposed cuts are not law yet.

Optimized Foods shows what businesses can do with research they can build on. The concern is what happens to the next idea when fewer people have the support to explore it.

For a grower, that could mean waiting longer for a new use for a crop byproduct. For a founder, it could mean a useful question about production costs goes unanswered.

Funding choices made now could determine whether innovative businesses thrive or fail. Yothers has shown the importance of public research for his business. Continued investment in federal grants helps more researchers find useful, industry-changing solutions.

FAQ: Fungi food

  • What are alternative foods?
  • Alternative foods resemble familiar foods or ingredients, such as meat or dairy, while delivering a similar nutritional profile. Some are designed to provide more nutrients, cut calories or fat, use fewer natural resources, or cost less for producers to make and consumers to buy.
  • What are myco-foods?
  • Myco-foods are a category of sustainable, nutrient-dense foods derived from edible fungi and their root-like network, mycelium (which UC Davis is helping define). Optimized Foods grows myco-foods as small balls, or pellets, of edible fungi that can be processed into products like caviar and alternative fats, with a wide range of textures, colors and flavors.
  • Are fungi foods sustainable?
  • Yes. Producing myco-foods requires fewer resources and generates fewer greenhouse gas emissions than traditional animal farming. Myco-foods are also grown from the nutrients of agricultural byproducts like coffee grounds and almond hulls that would otherwise be tossed out as food waste.
  • What is mycoprotein?
  • Mycoprotein (literally “protein from fungi”) is the commercialized, protein-rich food ingredient made from mycelium that you can find in alternative meat products like Quorn.
  • Is fungi a plant or an animal?
  • It’s neither. Fungi belong to a completely separate biological kingdom from plants and animals. This means mycoprotein is in a different category from soy, pea or wheat proteins.
