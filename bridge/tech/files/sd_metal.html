A simple metal is hiding a strange new form of magnetism

Date:
November 24, 2026
Source:
Max Planck Institute for Chemical Physics of Solids
Summary:
Scientists have discovered a strange form of magnetism in an ordinary-looking metal that has no overall magnetization yet profoundly changes how electrons behave. The breakthrough offers a promising new path toward understanding exotic quantum materials and developing future electronic technologies.
Share:
Facebook Twitter Pinterest LinkedIN Email
FULL STORY

A surprisingly simple metal has revealed an unusual form of magnetism that could open new possibilities for quantum technology. Researchers have found compelling evidence of a rare magnetic state in which electrons behave in unexpected ways, even though the material produces no overall magnetization.

The material, known as europium silicide (EuSi), contains an intricate magnetic structure that influences how electrons move and how their spins are oriented. This behavior is associated with a phenomenon called p-wave magnetism, an emerging area of research that could eventually help scientists develop new approaches to controlling electronic properties.

The discovery is particularly significant because EuSi is a relatively simple metallic compound. Unlike many materials previously considered for studying this exotic form of magnetism, it offers researchers a practical way to investigate the underlying physics in detail.

An Unexpected Discovery in a Simple Metal

The research began about three years ago at the Max Planck Institute for Chemical Physics of Solids (MPI CPfS) in Dresden, Germany. Dr. Erjian Cheng successfully grew high-quality single crystals of EuSi, providing the material needed for detailed experiments.

When researchers first examined the crystals using photoemission techniques, which probe the energies and behavior of electrons inside materials, they noticed something unexpected. The magnetic arrangement of the atoms had a remarkably strong influence on the electronic band structure, which describes the energy levels electrons can occupy and helps determine how a material conducts electricity.

That observation raised an intriguing possibility. Could this simple compound contain an unconventional form of magnetism in which the spin of an electron is connected to the direction it travels?

"When we first measured the electronic structure of EuSi, we were surprised by the exceptionally large effect of magnetism on the electrons," explains Cheng. "That observation led us to investigate whether this simple compound could host a much more unusual magnetic state. The combination of crystal growth, neutron scattering and spin-resolved spectroscopy now provides compelling evidence for p-wave magnetism."

Answering that question required an international collaboration involving 38 scientists from 19 institutions. Participants included researchers from ShanghaiTech University, the Hong Kong University of Science and Technology, the Max Planck Institute for the Physics of Complex Systems and Oak Ridge National Laboratory.

A Strange Form of Magnetism That Cancels Itself Out

Ordinary magnets, such as those attached to refrigerators, typically rely on a magnetic arrangement called ferromagnetism. In these materials, many electron spins align in the same direction, producing an overall magnetic field.

EuSi behaves very differently.

At temperatures between approximately 33 and 62 kelvin, the magnetic moments of its europium atoms organize themselves into a repeating spiral pattern. These temperatures correspond to roughly minus 400 to minus 348 degrees Fahrenheit.

Each europium atom has a substantial magnetic moment, but their orientations balance one another across the spiral. As a result, the material has no net magnetization in this magnetic phase.

However, the absence of overall magnetization does not mean magnetism has stopped influencing the electrons. In fact, the researchers found that the spiral arrangement produces a highly unusual electronic structure.

In the proposed p-wave magnetic state, electrons traveling in opposite directions have opposite spin polarizations. Electron spin is a quantum property that gives electrons magnetic characteristics, although it should not be understood as an electron literally spinning like a tiny ball.

The relationship between an electron's direction of motion and its spin is called an odd-parity spin texture. This means that reversing the electron's momentum also reverses the associated spin polarization.

Other materials can display spin-dependent energy differences because of a phenomenon known as spin-orbit coupling, which connects an electron's motion to its spin. In EuSi, however, the unusual spin structure appears to arise mainly from interactions between magnetic moments and the specific symmetry of their spiral arrangement.

Experiments Reveal Evidence of Rare P-Wave Magnetism

To determine whether EuSi truly exhibits the predicted magnetic state, the scientists combined several sophisticated experimental methods with theoretical calculations.

First, they used neutron diffraction, a technique that reveals how atoms and magnetic moments are arranged inside a material. These measurements confirmed that the europium magnetic moments form a spiral structure with the symmetry required for p-wave magnetism.

Next, the researchers examined the material using angle-resolved photoemission spectroscopy. This method measures the energies and momenta of electrons, allowing scientists to map the electronic structure.

The results showed that magnetic ordering substantially reorganizes the electron energy levels. In particular, the team observed magnetically driven energy separations exceeding 100 millielectronvolts, indicating an unusually strong influence of magnetism on electron behavior.

The most decisive evidence came from spin-resolved photoemission spectroscopy, which allowed the researchers to examine electron spin polarization directly.

They observed that the sign of the spin polarization changed across successive magnetic Brillouin zones. These zones are regions of momentum space used by physicists to describe how electrons behave within a repeating magnetic structure.

The team also found that spin polarization became much weaker near locations where theoretical calculations predicted the p-wave spin texture should disappear.

Taken together, the measurements provide compelling experimental evidence consistent with the predicted p-wave magnetic state in EuSi.

A Hidden Magnetic Spiral With Two Different Orientations

The researchers uncovered another unusual feature of EuSi involving chirality, a scientific term describing handedness.

A familiar example is the difference between a left hand and a right hand. They are mirror images, but they cannot be perfectly superimposed.

Something similar happens with the magnetic spiral inside EuSi. Although the underlying crystal has a centrosymmetric structure, meaning its atomic arrangement is unchanged by inversion through a central point, the magnetic spiral can twist in either of two opposite directions.

These different spiral orientations create magnetic regions, or domains, with opposite handedness.

Using magnetic force microscopy and spatially resolved photoemission spectroscopy, the scientists identified domains measuring approximately ten micrometers across. For perspective, ten micrometers is about one-tenth the thickness of a typical human hair.

When the researchers examined these regions using circularly polarized light, they detected different electronic responses depending on the handedness of the magnetic spiral.

This finding demonstrates that the direction in which the magnetic structure twists is linked to the material's electronic properties.

"What makes EuSi particularly exciting is that an unconventional electronic spin structure emerges from the magnetic order of a chemically simple metal," says Prof. Claudia Felser, Director at MPI CPfS. "This demonstrates how the discovery of new materials can reveal entirely new electronic phenomena. EuSi now gives us an experimental platform to investigate how magnetic symmetry and chirality can be used to control electronic properties, with potential implications for future spintronics and unconventional superconductivity."

Why This Discovery Matters for Quantum Technology

One of the biggest challenges in studying p-wave magnetism has been finding materials that display the necessary magnetic properties while remaining practical for detailed experiments.

Earlier candidates have included insulating materials, as well as metals that develop the required magnetic order only at extremely low temperatures or have complicated symmetries that make their behavior difficult to interpret.

EuSi offers a particularly useful combination of characteristics.

It is a stoichiometric metal, meaning its constituent elements occur in a fixed chemical ratio. Researchers can also grow high-quality single crystals of the material, and its spiral magnetic arrangement has the symmetry associated with p-wave magnetism.

Together, these features make EuSi a promising experimental platform for exploring how exotic magnetic states influence the behavior of electrons.

Theoretical studies suggest that metallic p-wave magnets could display several remarkable properties. For example, they may allow electrons with particular spin orientations to carry electrical currents without requiring an external magnetic field.

They could also produce strong changes in spin polarization when an electric current is applied, potentially offering new ways to manipulate electronic behavior.

These possibilities are especially relevant to spintronics, a field of technology that seeks to use electron spin, rather than electrical charge alone, to process and store information.

Another intriguing possibility involves unconventional superconductivity, in which electrical current flows without resistance through superconducting states that differ from those found in conventional superconductors.

Importantly, these predicted effects have not yet been demonstrated in EuSi. The discovery does not establish that the material can already perform these functions, but it gives researchers a promising system in which to test the predictions.

More broadly, the findings highlight how much of a material's behavior can remain hidden beneath an apparently simple chemical composition.

A solid's properties depend on more than the elements it contains or how its atoms are positioned. The arrangement of magnetic moments, the symmetries they create, and the quantum behavior of electrons can also produce entirely unexpected effects.

By revealing compelling evidence of p-wave magnetism in EuSi, researchers have gained a new opportunity to investigate these relationships and explore how unusual magnetic structures might eventually be used to control the electronic properties of future materials.
